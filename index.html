Can a search loop beat random sampling at placing two hydrogen bonds in a de novo enzyme pocket, on two 12 GB GPUs over a weekend?

  • The search wins, and by a lot. Fresh random seeds clear the geometry gate 6.2% of the time. Partial re-diffusion gets 14.6%. Re-sequencing the same backbone gets 42.5%. Search against random is 21.9% vs 6.2%, Fisher p ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁴, and the ordering replicated across two batches.
  • Getting both bonds at once is the hard part. Each lands 10–37% alone, both together about 6%. Variant 12 put 14 of 60 on one bond and 6 of 60 on the other and 0 on both, against 1.4 expected if they were independent. So adding more backbones doesn't really help much. That's the bit that needs fixing.
  • Letting the loop run 3 cycles instead of 1 took scored designs from 63/830 to 142/1,122 and raised the best score in 7 of 11 variants. At 1 cycle, a variant that missed on cycle 0 got no search at all and just ended up doing random sampling.
  • 3 of the 5 score terms are noise. I ran a seed-to-seed control and compared the spread within one design to the spread between designs. Both angle terms and the donor distance sit at a ratio near or above 1, so they cannot separate designs. The brief fixed the angle weight at 0.40, so I left it alone and noted in the log that the data says otherwise.

Can a method find members of an anti-CRISPR family it has never seen?

  • Published predictors don't test that. They hold out random members of families they already trained on, which measures interpolation inside known families. I hold out all 76 families in turn, with the folds frozen in a manifest so anyone's embedding can be scored on exactly the folds my numbers used.
  • Homology search falls apart when the family is genuinely new. BLASTp 0.566 AUROC, phmmer 0.531. Chance is 0.500.
  • ESM-2 650M with a logistic probe: 0.831 AUROC and 0.430 AP, against a random baseline of 0.091.
  • 3B is worse than 650M. Both metrics, all three readouts, each peaking at 650M on its own. It's a real curve with a maximum in the middle.
  • 650M's best layer is its last one, which is the opposite of the usual advice. And taking the final layer as a default costs 0.053 AUROC at 150M and 0.043 at 3B. It happens to be right at exactly one model size.

Can a designed pocket pick a synthetic amino acid over the canonical residue it looks like? Binding on its own proves nothing. What counts is the size of the gap.

  • My first target doesn't work, and the volumes show why. Phenylalanine fits entirely inside the 4-fluorophenylalanine envelope: 0.0 Å³ of it sticks out. A pocket moulded to one accepts the other with no steric penalty at all.
  • LigandMPNN had already told me this before I measured it. Sequence choice moved between the two by 0.0034 bits, against 0.60 bits for switching whole-ligand context on and off — 180× more. So LigandMPNN wasn't failing at the job. There was just no signal there to pick up.
  • So I switched to 2-naphthylalanine: 6.0× the surplus volume and 2.2 Å more reach from CB. Worth roughly 1.2 kcal/mol, or about 10× in Kd.
  • Tryptophan is the one that actually matters here. I gave Trp its best shot — scanning χ2 for the rotamer with maximum overlap — and it still loses on three independent counts: 19.7 Å³ of surplus it has to clash with, 32.5 Å³ of contact it can't make, and its N–H landing 0.56 Å inside the envelope, where the pocket will have put apolar surface. Burying that donor costs 1–3 kcal/mol and doesn't depend on volume at all. That's the term the design objective should be written around.

How wrong is the shortcut everyone uses to predict whether a chemically modified peptide gets presented to the immune system?

  • The shortcut is to pretend the modification isn't there — swap the modified residue for its nearest normal one and run a standard predictor. Someone proposed it, nobody checked it against measured binding, and it stuck. So I did, on 154 peptides with measured IC50s, under two different mapping schemes so the answer isn't an artefact of one choice.
  • It ranks things fine — Spearman 0.807 in log space. If all you need is to sort candidates, use it.
  • The actual numbers are a different story, and most of the time it isn't predicting anything at all. In 135 of 154 rows the swapped peptide comes out identical to the native one, so the prediction is literally "the modification changes nothing". On the 58 of those with a measured number that's within 2× twenty-eight times, 2–10× twenty times, and off by more than tenfold 10 times.
  • Worst single case: a chlorinated tyrosine in SHLVEALYLV. Predicted 9.79 nM, measured 1066 nM. 109× out.

How I run things
Freeze the folds before you let a model near them. Run the noise control first. If the score can't tell two designs apart, the search isn't doing anything. Write down what you got wrong and leave it there. My logs have retractions in them on purpose. A missing number raises. It never quietly becomes 0.0.
